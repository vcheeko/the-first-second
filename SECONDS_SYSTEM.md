# THE FIRST SECOND — Seconds System v0.2

## Status

**Canonical mechanics layer: v0.2 / pre-prototype.**

This document defines the current safe baseline for the seconds mechanic. Exact numerical balancing is intentionally left open until a prototype can test it.

## 1. Core principle

Seconds are the player's relationship to local reality.

They are not merely health, money, score, age, or a conventional countdown. A player's remaining seconds influence how strongly that player is bound to the laws, identity model, and physical state of the simulation they currently inhabit.

The core inversion is:

- **more seconds** generally mean greater local stability;
- **fewer seconds** generally mean greater exposure to anomalies and boundaries between simulations.

Therefore, maximizing time is not automatically the same as maximizing freedom.

## 2. The master clock

The first public game timeline begins with the **first second after the Big Bang**.

That event establishes the original master-time reference used by the project. A simulation created later may have its own local clock and its own first second, but its time can still be related to the larger simulation tree.

The game must distinguish at least:

- **master time** — chronology measured from the foundational First Second;
- **simulation time** — time experienced inside a particular simulation;
- **player seconds** — the player's current temporal relationship to the simulation they inhabit.

These values are related but are not required to advance at the same rate.

## 3. Player entry

A player enters the playable experience with a finite number of player seconds.

The initial public design starts players on or from an Earth-like physical layer, but future simulations may permit different origins.

The exact starting value is not fixed in v0.2. It should eventually be determined by game balance, identity/state, the simulation entered, and any inherited history.

## 4. Seconds and state of existence

A player is not permanently bound to a human body.

Depending on remaining seconds and the laws of the active simulation, a player may exist as:

- biological matter;
- synthetic or machine matter;
- energy;
- light or light-like state;
- information;
- consciousness or distributed consciousness;
- metaphase / non-classical state;
- another form that only exists under the rules of a particular simulation.

A change of simulation may therefore require a change of form.

## 5. Stability gradient

The seconds system should behave as a **gradient**, not as a simple set of hard levels.

Conceptually:

```text
HIGH SECONDS
│
│  stable identity
│  stable matter
│  strong local physics
│  weak cross-simulation perception
│
├────────────── local reality boundary
│
│  rising anomalies
│  greater state flexibility
│  cross-simulation signals
│  increased transition probability
│
LOW SECONDS
│
└── 0 seconds: unresolved boundary state
```

The prototype may later use thresholds internally, but the player should experience a continuous change rather than an obvious arcade-style level ladder.

## 6. Low-second effects

As player seconds decrease, the game may progressively permit or increase:

- perception of impossible or contradictory events;
- detection of other simulation layers;
- memory leakage between timelines;
- temporary loss of conventional physical constraints;
- transformation into non-physical states;
- probability of crossing a simulation boundary;
- interaction with entities that are not normally observable;
- encounters with or traces of The First Second.

These effects are probabilistic and simulation-dependent. Low seconds do not guarantee teleportation.

## 7. Crossing a simulation boundary

Teleportation is not ordinary movement through space.

A simulation crossing is a transition between rule systems.

A successful crossing may require compatibility between:

1. the player's remaining seconds;
2. the player's current state of existence;
3. the destination simulation's rules;
4. identity continuity;
5. memory encoding;
6. available transition path or anomaly.

A player that cannot exist under the destination rules may need to transform, be partially reconstructed, lose information, or fail the transition.

## 8. Memory cost and continuity

Memory is part of the seconds system.

Crossing simulations can create uncertainty about whether the arriving player is exactly the same entity, a continuation, a reconstruction, or a branch.

Possible consequences include:

- partial memory loss;
- duplicated memories;
- memories from another branch;
- memories that refer to events not yet experienced locally;
- identity fragmentation;
- preserved memory with changed physical form.

The game should treat this as a mechanic, not only as narrative exposition.

## 9. The First Second

**The First Second** is a special exception to ordinary player constraints.

Current canonical properties:

- it is associated with the foundational first second;
- it can retain memory across multiple times and simulations;
- it can move between simulation layers in ways ordinary players cannot;
- it may possess memories that are future events relative to a local observer;
- it is not fully bound to one local timeline or one physical form.

This does not mean The First Second is omnipotent. Its limitations remain deliberately open for later design so that it cannot become a trivial solution to every game problem.

## 10. Zero seconds

`0 seconds` is **not automatically GAME OVER**.

Zero is a boundary condition whose meaning depends on the player's state and simulation.

It may lead to:

- death or termination;
- transformation;
- loss of local physical form;
- transition outside local time;
- simulation crossing;
- identity branching;
- an unknown state that cannot be represented by ordinary local physics.

The game must not promise that reaching zero is beneficial. Zero should remain dangerous, uncertain, and potentially irreversible inside gameplay.

## 11. Gaining and losing seconds

The full economy is not locked yet.

Safe design constraints for later prototyping:

- seconds may be gained, lost, transferred, distorted, or reinterpreted;
- gaining seconds must have trade-offs so the optimal strategy is not simply infinite accumulation;
- losing seconds must not be equivalent to taking ordinary damage;
- no real-money mechanic should directly buy existential advantage during the concept/prototype phase;
- cross-simulation exploits must be treated as part of the simulation model rather than ignored.

## 12. What the player is trying to prove

The apparent early objective may be to survive and preserve seconds.

The deeper objective is to reach or create a simulation sophisticated enough to test whether the player's own reality is itself simulated.

The game should never falsely claim that this constitutes scientific proof about the real world. Within the game, the player is performing an experiment on the game's reality structure.

## 13. Prototype questions

The next prototype should answer these questions empirically rather than by documentation alone:

1. What starting seconds create meaningful pressure without becoming a normal countdown timer?
2. How quickly should stability change as seconds decrease?
3. Which low-second anomalies are readable to a player without explaining the system directly?
4. What is the first mechanically testable simulation-crossing event?
5. What information must survive a transition for the player to feel continuous identity?
6. What prevents deliberate rapid depletion from always being the optimal strategy?
7. What limitations keep The First Second mysterious rather than all-powerful?

## 14. Non-goals of v0.2

This version does **not** lock:

- exact numeric thresholds;
- final spawn values;
- monetization;
- final death rules;
- final win condition;
- final physics model;
- game engine or network architecture.

Those decisions should be made only after the mechanics can be tested.
